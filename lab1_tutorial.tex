\section*{Objective}

In Lab 0 you profiled the four applications and chose \emph{one kernel} worth
building custom hardware for. Lab 1 is where you build it --- but \emph{not}
inside X-HEEP yet. You implement the accelerator as \textbf{standalone RTL},
and you prove it correct with a \textbf{self-checking testbench} that runs on
\emph{both} an open-source simulator (Verilator) and a commercial one
(QuestaSim), checked against a \emph{golden model} of your kernel.

The rule for this stage: no SoC, no bus fabric, no software driver. Just your
accelerator, a testbench that feeds it inputs and checks its outputs, and the
same numbers coming out of both simulators. Getting a kernel \emph{correct} in
isolation --- and building the harness that tells you it is correct --- is the
foundation everything else (optimization in Lab 2, integration in Lab 3) sits
on. This document describes what to do and the toolkit you are given to do it.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Worked example.} The scaffold ships a complete testbench for
\code{matmul} over an \emph{OBI + CSR} interface. If you chose a different
kernel or interface, you keep the same structure --- golden vectors, transactors,
self-checking testbench --- and adapt the pieces. The parts you reuse unchanged
(the CSR driver, the golden-vector checker, the Makefile flow) are the same for
everyone.

\section{The interface contract}

Before any RTL, decide how your accelerator talks to the outside world. This is
the one thing the testbench and your design must agree on. Two building blocks:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{CSR / register interface} --- a small set of memory-mapped
        \emph{control/status/configuration} registers: start the accelerator,
        read back ``done'', configure sizes and pointers. This is
        \emph{control only}. Bulk matrix data does \emph{not} travel through
        here.
  \item \textbf{OBI data port} --- how the bulk data (the matrices) moves. You
        pick one of two styles:
        \begin{itemize}[nosep]
          \item \textbf{OBI master} (DMA-like): the accelerator itself reads A
                and B from memory and writes C back. The testbench provides the
                memory.
          \item \textbf{OBI slave}: the testbench pushes A and B in and reads C
                out over the bus; the accelerator is a passive target.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The shipped example is \textbf{OBI master}. Its register map lives in
\code{rtl/matmul\_accel\_pkg.sv} and is the contract the CSR driver relies on:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
Offset & Name & Meaning \\
\midrule
\code{0x00} & \code{CTRL}   & bit 0 = \code{start} (write-1 pulse) \\
\code{0x04} & \code{STATUS} & bit 0 = \code{done} (RO), bit 1 = \code{busy} (RO) \\
\code{0x08} & \code{N}      & matrix dimension (reset value 16) \\
\code{0x0C} & \code{A\_PTR} & byte base address of A in memory \\
\code{0x10} & \code{B\_PTR} & byte base address of B in memory \\
\code{0x14} & \code{C\_PTR} & byte base address of C in memory \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

You are free to change this map --- but if you do, update the CSR driver so the
testbench still programs you correctly.

\subsection{OBI: the bus underneath}

Whichever side you pick, the data port speaks \textbf{OBI} (Open Bus Interface),
the lightweight memory/peripheral bus the OpenHW Group specifies and X-HEEP uses
throughout. The wire bundle in \code{tb/obi\_if.sv} is not arbitrary --- every
signal is taken straight from the OBI specification. It is worth reading that
spec (the PDF at \url{https://github.com/openhwgroup/obi}) before you design the
master FSM, so the handshake becomes a protocol you understand rather than a
pattern you copy. The essential idea is a two-phase, \emph{decoupled} handshake:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Address phase.} The master raises \code{req} with an \code{addr}
        (plus \code{we}/\code{be}/\code{wdata} for a write). The slave accepts it
        on the cycle it asserts \code{gnt}: a beat is committed exactly on
        \code{req \&\& gnt}, and not before.
  \item \textbf{Response phase.} For a read, the data returns \emph{later},
        flagged by \code{rvalid} alongside \code{rdata} --- possibly several
        cycles after the grant, and it is the slave, not the master, that
        decides when.
\end{itemize}

Because the two phases are decoupled, a compliant master may issue the next
request while a previous read is still in flight. That pipelining is where an OBI
master earns its bandwidth --- and it is exactly the kind of optimization Lab 2
rewards. For Lab 1 a simple one-outstanding-request FSM (issue, wait for grant,
wait for \code{rvalid}, repeat) is correct and sufficient. The
\code{obi\_mem\_model} answers with a \emph{configurable} read latency
(\code{RLAT}) precisely so you can prove your handshake survives a slave that
does not respond in a single cycle; bump it and your FSM must still work. Read
the specification's channel and handshake sections for the full rules (byte
enables, error responses, and the optional signals X-HEEP does not use).

\section{Vendoring and reusing IP}

You already ran \code{make vendor} in Lab 0, but it is worth understanding what
it actually does, because you lean on it again here and in Lab 3.
\emph{Vendoring} takes a snapshot of an upstream repository --- pinned to an
exact revision --- and copies it into your tree, optionally applying local
patches. It is \emph{not} a git submodule: there is no live link to the remote,
nothing to \code{git submodule update}, and the build stays reproducible and
offline-buildable forever, because the exact bytes you built against live with
your project. The descriptor is a small \code{.vendor.hjson} naming the URL, the
\code{rev}, and any files to exclude or patches to apply; \code{util/vendor.py}
reads it and performs the copy. Pinning the \code{rev} is what keeps ``it built
last year'' true today.

The payoff for an accelerator designer is \emph{reuse}. You do not build every
FIFO, counter, and clock gate from scratch --- and you should not: the soundest
design reuses a proven, verified block instead of re-deriving one at 2am.
X-HEEP already vendors two libraries of exactly the sub-blocks an accelerator
needs, and because they sit in the vendored tree you can depend on them
directly:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{9.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
Library (VLNV) & What is in it \\
\midrule
\code{pulp-platform.org::common\_cells}
  & Reusable synthesizable RTL: FIFOs (\code{fifo\_v3}), counters,
    synchronizers, arbiters and round-robin, clock-domain-crossing primitives,
    one-hot/gray coders, register slices. The parts you would otherwise
    re-implement and re-verify. \\
\addlinespace
\code{pulp-platform.org::tech\_cells\_generic}
  & Technology-agnostic wrappers for cells that are otherwise process-specific:
    clock gates (\code{tc\_clk\_gating}), muxes, and SRAM macros
    (\code{tc\_sram}). Behavioural in simulation, swapped for real
    standard/macro cells at synthesis. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two teaching points. First, \emph{reach for these before hand-rolling}: if your
datapath wants an input FIFO to decouple the OBI reads from the MAC pipeline,
\code{fifo\_v3} already exists and is one instantiation away. Second,
\code{tech\_cells\_generic} is what keeps your RTL \emph{portable}: by
instantiating \code{tc\_clk\_gating} or \code{tc\_sram} rather than a
vendor-specific primitive, the same source elaborates in Verilator, maps in the
open-source synthesis flow, and maps again in the commercial one --- the two
flows you must compare in Lab 2. Hard-coding a foundry cell would break one of
them.

Making a library available is one line in your core file: add its VLNV to the
\code{depend:} list of the fileset that needs it, and FuseSoC resolves it from
the vendored X-HEEP.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sh]
filesets:
  rtl:
    depend:
      - pulp-platform.org::common_cells
      - pulp-platform.org::tech_cells_generic
    files:
      - rtl/matmul_accel.sv
    file_type: systemVerilogSource
\end{lstlisting}

The upstream sources and their documentation live at
\url{https://github.com/pulp-platform/common_cells} and
\url{https://github.com/pulp-platform/tech_cells_generic}.

\section{Generating the register file with reggen}

The CSR block from the interface contract --- the address decode, the read
multiplexer, the per-bit write enables, the read-only versus read-write versus
write-one-to-pulse semantics --- is mechanical, repetitive, and a classic place
for hardware and software to drift apart: you add a field in the RTL, forget the
matching C \code{\#define}, and the driver writes the wrong bit. The disciplined
way to build it is to \emph{not} hand-write it at all. You describe the registers
\emph{once} in a small HJSON file and a generator emits both the RTL and a
matching C header from that single source. Change the description, regenerate,
and the two can no longer disagree.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Use the X-HEEP version, not the mainstream one.} This is the one
warning to internalize. The tool is OpenTitan's \code{regtool} (commonly called
``reggen''), but X-HEEP ships its \emph{own vendored and patched copy}, wrapped
as a FuseSoC generator named \code{regtool} (VLNV
\code{xheep:util:reg-generator}). Do \emph{not} \code{pip install reggen} and run
the stock tool: the mainstream version may accept slightly different HJSON keys
and, more importantly, emit a different register-bus flavour than the
\code{reg\_interface} that X-HEEP expects. Always drive the copy that came with
your vendored \code{x-heep/}. The authoritative how-to is
\code{x-heep/docs/source/How\_to/RegGenerator.md}; the upstream reference for
field semantics --- what \code{swaccess} and \code{hwaccess} mean, the full
register and field grammar --- is the OpenTitan manual at
\url{https://opentitan.org/book/util/reggen/index.html}. Read the manual to
understand the fields, but defer to the X-HEEP wrapper for how you invoke it.

\subsection{The register description}

Your registers live in \code{data/matmul\_accel.hjson}. It declares a
\code{reg\_iface} device interface, a 32-bit register width, and then one entry
per register, each with its software access mode (\code{swaccess}), hardware
access mode (\code{hwaccess}), reset value, and fields. A trimmed version of the
\code{matmul\_accel} block:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sh]
{ name: "matmul_accel"
  bus_interfaces: [ { protocol: "reg_iface", direction: "device" } ]
  regwidth: "32"
  registers: [
    { name: "CTRL",   swaccess: "wo", hwaccess: "hro",
      desc: "Control: bit 0 = start (write-1 pulse)",
      fields: [ { bits: "0", name: "START" } ] }
    { name: "STATUS", swaccess: "ro", hwaccess: "hwo",
      desc: "Status: done, busy",
      fields: [ { bits: "0", name: "DONE" }, { bits: "1", name: "BUSY" } ] }
    { name: "N",      swaccess: "rw", hwaccess: "hro", resval: "16",
      fields: [ { bits: "31:0", name: "N" } ] }
    { name: "A_PTR",  swaccess: "rw", hwaccess: "hro",
      fields: [ { bits: "31:0", name: "A_PTR" } ] }
    // ... B_PTR, C_PTR likewise
  ]
}
\end{lstlisting}

The \code{swaccess}/\code{hwaccess} pair is the whole point: \code{CTRL.START} is
\code{wo} from software and \code{hro} (hardware-read-only) --- software pulses
it, hardware reads it --- while \code{STATUS} is \code{ro} from software and
\code{hwo} (hardware-write-only), because hardware produces \code{done}/\code{busy}
and software only observes them. Specify the modes correctly and the generator
builds exactly the right flops and enables for you.

\subsection{What it produces, and how the accelerator uses it}

Running the generator over \code{data/matmul\_accel.hjson} produces three
artefacts:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{9.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
Artefact & Role \\
\midrule
\code{rtl/matmul\_accel\_reg\_pkg.sv}
  & The generated package: the \code{reg2hw} and \code{hw2reg} struct types that
    carry each field between the CSR block and your logic. \\
\addlinespace
\code{rtl/matmul\_accel\_reg\_top.sv}
  & The CSR block itself: a \code{reg\_req} in / \code{reg\_rsp} out register
    interface on one side, and the \code{reg2hw}/\code{hw2reg} structs on the
    other. All the decode and read-mux you did not have to write. \\
\addlinespace
\code{sw/matmul\_accel\_regs.h}
  & The C header of register offsets and field masks the Lab 3 driver will
    \code{\#include} --- generated from the \emph{same} HJSON, so it cannot drift
    from the RTL. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

You then instantiate \code{matmul\_accel\_reg\_top} inside your accelerator and
wire it up: give it the CSR bus --- the very same register interface the
testbench's \code{reg\_driver} drives --- and it hands you \code{reg2hw} (read
\code{reg2hw.n}, \code{reg2hw.a\_ptr}, the \code{reg2hw.ctrl.start} pulse) and
accepts \code{hw2reg} (drive \code{hw2reg.status.done} and \code{.busy} from your
FSM). This is precisely why the register map in \code{rtl/matmul\_accel\_pkg.sv}
and the HJSON must describe the \emph{same} layout: the testbench programs
offsets defined in the package, and your hardware decodes them in the generated
block.

\subsection{The FuseSoC generator}

You never call the tool by hand. In FuseSoC a \emph{generator} is a build-time
program that \emph{produces source before elaboration} --- here, HJSON in,
SystemVerilog and a C header out --- declared in your \code{.core} file. You add
\code{xheep:util:reg-generator} as a dependency and a \code{generate:} block that
names the \code{regtool} generator and its parameters:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sh]
generate:
  matmul-accel-regs:
    generator: regtool
    parameters:
      name:     matmul_accel
      config:   data/matmul_accel.hjson
      rtl_dir:  rtl
      sw_path:  sw/matmul_accel_regs.h
      doc_path: sw/matmul_accel_regs.md

targets:
  default:
    filesets: [rtl]
    generate: [matmul-accel-regs]
\end{lstlisting}

All paths are relative to the directory of the \code{.core} file. Two commands
drive it:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
make vendor   # pull X-HEEP into ./x-heep -- brings the reggen generator + IPs
make regs     # run the regtool generator over data/matmul_accel.hjson
\end{lstlisting}

Run these inside the \code{core-v-mini-mcu} conda environment, the one that also
provides \code{fusesoc}. This is not a bespoke mechanism: it is exactly how
X-HEEP generates the registers for \emph{all} of its own peripherals ---
\code{soc\_ctrl}, \code{pad\_control}, and the rest --- and it is the same
\code{.core} and the same generator you carry into the SoC in Lab 3. Building the
register block this way now means it is already in the shape integration expects.

\section{The golden model}

A self-checking testbench needs a \emph{reference}: the correct outputs to
compare against. You generate them two ways, and they must agree.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Python golden} (\code{model/golden.py}) --- reproduces the
        kernel and writes \code{vectors/a.hex}, \code{b.hex},
        \code{c\_golden.hex} (one 32-bit word per line, row-major), the format
        the testbench loads with \code{\$readmemh}.
  \item \textbf{C dump from the real kernel} (\code{make host-vectors}) ---
        compiles the \emph{actual} Lab-0 C kernel for your host machine and
        dumps the buffers it reads and writes, using the \code{DUMP\_HEX(...)}
        macros in \code{model/dump.h}. On the X-HEEP target those macros
        compile to nothing, so the same C runs unchanged in Lab 3.
\end{enumerate}

The second route is the important one: it is how you obtain a golden for
\emph{whatever kernel you actually picked}, without re-implementing the
algorithm in a second language. Wrap the region you accelerate:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=clisting]
#include "dump.h"
...
DUMP_HEX("vectors/a.hex", &a[0][0], N*N);   // inputs the kernel reads
DUMP_HEX("vectors/b.hex", &b[0][0], N*N);
kernel();                                    // the code you accelerate
DUMP_HEX("vectors/c_golden.hex", &c[0][0], N*N);  // outputs it produced
\end{lstlisting}

For \code{matmul} both routes produce identical files (same checksum) --- that
cross-check is deliberate: if they disagree, one of your two references is
wrong before you have written a line of RTL.

\section{The testbench toolkit}

You do not write the testbench from scratch. You \emph{mount} one from a small
library of components, picking the transactors that match your interface. Each
lives in its own file under \code{tb/}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{6.3cm}@{}}
\toprule
File & Role & Use when \\
\midrule
\code{obi\_mem\_model.sv}  & OBI \emph{slave} RAM (backdoor load/read) & accel is OBI \emph{master} \\
\code{obi\_master\_bfm.sv} & OBI \emph{master} driver (\code{write/read\_word}) & accel is OBI \emph{slave} \\
\code{reg\_driver.sv}      & CSR write / read / poll & always \\
\code{vector\_check.sv}    & load golden, compare, report (in the TB) & always \\
\code{obi\_if.sv}, \code{reg\_if.sv} & interface wire bundles & always \\
\code{tb\_matmul\_accel.sv} & top testbench (wires the OBI-master case) & the file you edit \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two OBI components are mirror images --- you instantiate exactly \emph{one}:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Accelerator is an \textbf{OBI master}? It drives the bus; the testbench
        must \emph{answer}. Instantiate \code{obi\_mem\_model} --- a behavioural
        RAM that grants requests and returns data. The testbench backdoor-loads
        A and B into it and reads C back out of it.
  \item Accelerator is an \textbf{OBI slave}? It waits for the bus; the
        testbench must \emph{drive}. Instantiate \code{obi\_master\_bfm} and
        call \code{write\_word}/\code{read\_word} to push A/B in and read C out.
\end{itemize}

The \code{reg\_driver} and \code{vector\_check} are used unchanged either way.
The top testbench then reads like the description of the test itself:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=svlisting]
// preload inputs into the RAM (backdoor)
u_mem.load_hex(A_BASE, {vdir, "a.hex"}, WORDS);
u_mem.load_hex(B_BASE, {vdir, "b.hex"}, WORDS);

// program the accelerator over CSRs
u_regdrv.write(REG_N,     N);
u_regdrv.write(REG_A_PTR, A_BASE);
u_regdrv.write(REG_B_PTR, B_BASE);
u_regdrv.write(REG_C_PTR, C_BASE);
u_regdrv.write(REG_CTRL,  1 << CTRL_START_BIT);   // GO

// wait for completion, then compare C against the golden vector
u_regdrv.poll_bit(REG_STATUS, STATUS_DONE_BIT, TIMEOUT, ok);
for (int i = 0; i < WORDS; i++) got[i] = u_mem.word(C_BASE + 4*i);
errors = compare(got, {vdir, "c_golden.hex"}, WORDS);
\end{lstlisting}

The comparison happens \emph{inside} the testbench: \code{vector\_check} loads
\code{c\_golden.hex}, diffs it against what the accelerator produced, prints the
first mismatches, and the testbench exits \code{TEST PASSED} (status 0) or
\code{TEST FAILED} (non-zero). No external diff step.

\subsection{One testbench, both simulators --- and why not DPI}

The entire testbench is plain SystemVerilog. It compiles on \emph{both}
Verilator 5 (\code{--binary --timing}) and QuestaSim, so nothing is written
twice: the transactors are the single source of truth for both tools. A DPI
bridge (calling a C golden at simulation time) would also avoid duplication, but
here it buys nothing over precomputed \code{\$readmemh} vectors, so the scaffold
does not use it. If a future kernel needs a golden computed on the fly, DPI is
the hook you would add --- but you do not need it for Lab 1.

\section{What you implement}

Exactly one file: \code{rtl/matmul\_accel.sv}. It ships as a stub that
elaborates but computes nothing (so the testbench correctly reports a mismatch
until you fill it in). For the reference OBI-master contract you build:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{CSR block} --- decode \code{reg\_addr\_i}; hold \code{N},
        \code{A\_PTR}, \code{B\_PTR}, \code{C\_PTR}; latch the \code{START}
        pulse; expose \code{STATUS.\{done,busy\}}; always assert
        \code{reg\_ready\_o}.
  \item \textbf{FSM + datapath} --- on \code{START}, stream A and B from memory
        over the OBI master port, compute $C = A \cdot B$ in \code{int32},
        write C back, then raise \code{STATUS.done}.
  \item \textbf{OBI master handshake} --- assert \code{req}/\code{addr}
        (\code{+we/be/wdata} for writes); a beat is accepted on \code{gnt};
        read data returns later on \code{rvalid}/\code{rdata}.
\end{enumerate}

Build the \emph{naive} version first --- one multiply-accumulate, the plain
triple loop. Correctness now; the optimization (pipelining, folding, a MAC
array) is Lab 2's job, and it will be measured \emph{against} this baseline.

\section{Commands}

Run everything from the \code{lab1/} directory.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
make vendor         # pull X-HEEP into ./x-heep (reggen generator + IPs)
make regs           # generate the CSR block from data/matmul_accel.hjson

make vectors        # golden vectors from the Python model

make questa         # QuestaSim / ModelSim: same testbench, same result
make sim-fusesoc    # same run through the FuseSoC .core (a Lab 3 preview)

\end{lstlisting}

\section{Reading the output}

A run ends in exactly one of two lines. Passing:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
$ make sim
[tb] vectors from 'vectors/', N=16 (256 words/matrix)
TEST PASSED: 256/256 words match
\end{lstlisting}

Failing (the shipped stub, until you implement the datapath):

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
$ make sim
[tb] vectors from 'vectors/', N=16 (256 words/matrix)
  MISMATCH [0]: got 00000000  exp 000004b0
  ...
TEST FAILED: 256/256 words mismatch
\end{lstlisting}

If the accelerator never sets \code{STATUS.done}, the CSR poll times out
instead (\code{TEST FAILED: STATUS.done not set within ... cycles}) --- that
means your FSM never finished (or never started). Use \code{make waves} to see
whether the OBI/CSR activity matches what you expect; waveforms are a
\textbf{debugging aid only} here, not a graded artifact.

Both simulators must give the \emph{same} result. That agreement --- open-source
